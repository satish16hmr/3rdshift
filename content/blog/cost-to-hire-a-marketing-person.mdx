Hiring a marketing person will cost you somewhere between $65,000 and $120,000 per year, all-in, depending on the role. But that range assumes everything goes right. The salary you'll post on LinkedIn is the smallest number in the equation. Once you load it with taxes, benefits, tools, ramp time, and management overhead, the true cost to hire a marketing person is typically 1.5x to 2x the base salary before they've produced a single result.

Most small-business owners figure this out six months after signing the offer letter.

This article breaks down what you'll actually spend, compares every real option available in 2026, and tells you honestly which one fits your stage of business.

## The Base Salary: What Marketing Roles Actually Pay

These are general market ranges. Actual figures vary by city, industry, company size, and the specific candidate you're trying to close.

### Marketing Coordinator (entry-level, 0-3 years experience)

Base salary range: $42,000 to $58,000 per year. This person can execute. They'll schedule posts, write first drafts, manage the calendar, and keep campaigns moving. What they can't do is own strategy. You'll still be making the calls, which means your time goes into this hire.

### Marketing Manager (mid-level, 3-7 years experience)

Base salary range: $65,000 to $90,000 per year. This is the role most growing businesses actually want. Someone who can build a plan, run channels, and report results without hand-holding. This is also the most competitive band to hire in. Good candidates have options.

### Marketing Director (senior, 7+ years)

Base salary range: $95,000 to $140,000 per year. At this level you're paying for strategic ownership. They should run the function while you focus elsewhere. But for most small businesses under $5 million in annual revenue, this is buying more seniority than the work requires.

None of these numbers include what it actually costs to have that person on payroll.

## The True Loaded Cost of a Marketing Hire

The loaded cost is the real number every founder should build their budget around before posting a job.

This is what gets stacked on top of base salary:

### Payroll taxes (7.65% minimum)

FICA alone is 7.65% of wages up to the Social Security wage base. Add state unemployment taxes, and you're typically looking at 8-12% depending on your state. On a $75,000 salary, that's $6,000 to $9,000 before benefits touch the spreadsheet.

### Health insurance

Employer contributions typically run $400 to $700 per month per employee for a single-coverage plan in 2026. Family plans push $900 to $1,400. Call it $5,000 to $10,000 per year for a single employee.

### Tools and software

A working marketing stack is not cheap. Expect to add $300 to $800 per month in software: a CRM, an email platform, an SEO tool, a social scheduler, a design tool, maybe a paid analytics layer. Many small businesses don't budget this at all because they assume the person comes with tools. They don't.

### Recruiting cost

If you use a recruiter, standard contingency fees run 15-20% of first-year salary. On a $75,000 hire, that's $11,250 to $15,000 gone before day one. Even if you post on LinkedIn and do it yourself, you're spending 15-30 hours screening, interviewing, and reference-checking. Put a number on your own time.

### Onboarding and ramp

Plan for 30 to 60 days before they're moving independently, and 90 to 120 days before they're operating at full output. You'll spend real time during that window explaining your business, your customers, and what you've tried. That's no complaint about the hire; onboarding just works that way.

### The all-in multiplier

Stack all of this up and a $75,000 marketing manager typically costs $105,000 to $120,000 annually. The 1.5x to 1.7x multiplier is consistent across most small business situations. Some founders get to 2x once management time is priced.

## The Hidden Cost Nobody Budgets: Time-to-Productivity

The loaded cost number above still leaves out the thing that hurts most in year one: the time before real output.

Most marketing hires don't reach full productivity until month four or five. Nobody's at fault for that. It takes time to understand the product, the customer, the voice, the existing content, the tools setup, and which channels have been tried. A good hire asks a lot of questions early. That's actually a good sign.

But during that ramp, you're paying full salary while output is partial.

And there's a second cost hiding here: your own management time. Even a strong, experienced marketer needs direction, feedback, alignment, and context from you. Budget 3 to 5 hours per week minimum, especially in the first six months. For most founders, that time costs more than the benefits line item.

The honest math on a $75,000 marketing manager in year one:

- All-in loaded cost: approximately $110,000
- Months at partial output (roughly 4): approximately $37,000 spent before full capacity
- Your management time at, say, 4 hrs/week for 52 weeks: 208 hours

That's the real number you're making a decision against.

## Your Three Options in 2026

You're not choosing between "hire or don't hire" anymore. There are three genuinely different options, each with a different cost structure, speed, and fit.

### Option 1: Full-Time Employee

When it makes sense: You're doing $1.5M+ in annual revenue, marketing is a repeatable function you need owned long-term, and you have the management bandwidth to develop someone. A full-time hire gives you depth, continuity, and institutional knowledge. Nobody else will learn your brand voice as well.

When it doesn't: You're pre-revenue or under $500K, you need results in 30 days, or you're not sure yet which channels matter. A full-time hire at that stage is an expensive bet on an unproven channel mix.

Monthly cost range: $8,500 to $13,000 all-in for a mid-level hire.

### Option 2: Agency or Freelancer

When it makes sense: You know the channel (SEO, paid ads, email) and want execution without managing a person. Agencies bring team depth and can start fast. Freelancers are more flexible and often cheaper.

The trade-offs: Agencies typically charge $3,000 to $8,000 per month for small-business retainers, and you're one of many clients. Freelancers run $50 to $150 per hour depending on specialization, which sounds manageable until you need consistent output across multiple channels. Neither option builds internal capability.

What you don't get: A single person who cares about all of your marketing as a whole. You get the deliverable you contracted, not strategic ownership.

Monthly cost range: $2,000 to $8,000 depending on scope and quality.

### Option 3: AI Workforce

This is the option most small-business owners in 2026 haven't priced out yet.

AI workforce platforms give you a team of specialized AI team members across content, social, SEO, email, and research functions, coordinated and producing output at a fraction of a human salary. ## Which Option Fits Your Stage

### Under $500K annual revenue

You need output, not overhead. A full-time hire at this stage competes directly with payroll for your core team. Start with an AI workforce to get consistent content, email, and social running. Add a specialist freelancer for paid ads if that channel is proven. Build the foundation without the fixed cost.

### $500K to $1.5M annual revenue

You've found some traction and need to scale what's working. A part-time or contract marketing manager (15-20 hours/week, $3,000 to $5,000/month) gives you strategy and prioritization without full-time overhead. Pair it with an AI workforce for execution. This is often the most efficient combination at this stage.

### $1.5M to $5M annual revenue

Now the full-time hire starts making economic sense. You need someone who can own the channel mix, run experiments, and build toward a real team. At this revenue level, a strong marketing manager should generate measurable return. Budget the loaded cost honestly and set clear output expectations in the first 90 days.

### $5M+

You're past the "can we afford marketing?" question. The real issue is building the function efficiently. That usually means a mix: a marketing director or VP, one or two specialists, an AI workforce for production volume, and an agency for one specialized channel like paid search or PR.

## FAQ

### How much does a marketing manager cost a small business?

A marketing manager typically costs $65,000 to $90,000 in base salary. Once you add payroll taxes, health insurance, tools, and recruiting costs, the true all-in expense runs $95,000 to $130,000 per year. That's before accounting for the 3-5 months of ramp time where output is partial. Most small businesses underestimate this by 30 to 40 percent when they first build the budget.

### Is it cheaper to hire an agency or a full-time marketing employee?

Agencies are almost always cheaper in year one, with retainers typically running $2,000 to $8,000 per month versus $8,500 to $13,000 all-in for a full-time employee. The catch: agencies don't build internal capability, they don't own your whole strategy, and the quality varies widely. For businesses with one or two channels to scale, agencies make sense. For businesses that need broad marketing ownership, eventually a full-time hire wins.

### Can AI replace a marketing hire?

Not entirely. AI workforce platforms are excellent at production: content, social copy, email sequences, SEO research, and first-draft work across channels. What they don't do is develop original strategy, build brand relationships, or make judgment calls that require deep business context. The most practical answer is that an AI workforce can replace a significant portion of what a junior-to-mid-level marketing hire would do, at a fraction of the cost, while a senior hire becomes more valuable when their time isn't swallowed by production tasks.

### What's the minimum to get marketing done?

If budget is the main constraint, an AI workforce subscription at $29 to $79 per month billed annually can cover content creation, social scheduling, email copy, and basic SEO execution. Add a freelance strategist at $1,500 to $2,500 per month to set direction and review performance, and you have a functional marketing operation for under $3,000 per month. That's a viable setup for businesses under $1M in annual revenue that need consistent output without full-time overhead.

### What costs do most small businesses forget when hiring marketing?

The three most commonly missed costs are the recruiting fee (15-20% of salary if you use a recruiter), the software stack ($300 to $800 per month that most new hires need to do the job), and the founder's own time during the ramp period. That last one doesn't show up in any budget line but it's real. Four hours a week for five months is 80+ hours of your time that isn't spent on other things.

### When should I hire a marketing person full-time instead of using an agency or AI?

A full-time hire makes sense when you have at least $1.5M in annual revenue, you've identified the channels that work, and you need someone who can own the function long-term rather than execute individual deliverables. If you're still figuring out which channels matter or you need results in the next 30 days, a full-time hire is the slowest and most expensive way to find out.

### How do I calculate the true cost of hiring marketing for my business?

Start with the base salary for the role you're considering. Multiply by 1.5 to get a conservative loaded cost estimate, or 1.7 to 2.0 if benefits in your state are expensive or you're using a recruiter. Then price out the software stack you'll need to add. Finally, estimate 4 hours per week of your own management time for the first six months and put a dollar value on it. Add those numbers together and you have a realistic first-year cost for the hire.

The true cost to hire a marketing person in 2026 is not the salary on the job posting. It's the salary, plus taxes, benefits, tools, recruiting, ramp time, and your own hours. That total almost always lands 50 to 100 percent above the base number.

The real question isn't what a marketing hire costs. It's what you're paying per output, per channel, per result. A $110,000 all-in hire who takes five months to ramp has a very different cost-per-outcome than a $199/month AI workforce that ships content on day one.
