# MODULE 1
## What Is a Dashboard — and Why Should You Care?

**Duration:** 20 minutes
**Format:** Facilitator-led discussion + individual reflection

---

### The Core Idea

A dashboard is a visual display of information. It pulls data from one or more sources and shows it in charts, numbers, and tables — all in one place. Think of it like the dashboard in your car. You don't need to understand how the engine works to know that the fuel gauge is low or that you're going 20 miles over the speed limit. The information is right in front of you, translated into something you can act on.

Business dashboards work the same way. They translate raw data — thousands of rows of numbers — into visuals you can scan quickly and understand.

### What Dashboards Are For

Dashboards serve three main purposes:

**Monitoring.** They show you what's happening right now, or over a recent period. Sales volume today. Website visitors this week. Customer complaints this month.

**Analyzing.** They help you understand *why* something is happening. Sales dropped — but was it across all products or just one? Was it everywhere or only one region?

**Deciding.** They give you the information you need to take action. Do we need more staff on Fridays? Is this promotion working? Should we stock more of this product?

### What Dashboards Are Not

Dashboards don't make decisions for you. They surface information. You still have to interpret it, apply judgment, and decide what to do. A dashboard that shows a 10% drop in sales isn't telling you to panic — it's inviting you to investigate.

They also aren't always right. Data can be incomplete, delayed, or mislabeled. Part of becoming a confident dashboard user is learning when to trust what you see and when to ask questions.

# MODULE 2
## Reading Charts and Metrics

**Duration:** 35 minutes
**Format:** Guided instruction + partner activity

---

### Why This Matters

Most dashboards use the same handful of chart types over and over. Once you know what each one is designed to show, you can read any dashboard — even one you've never seen before — with confidence.

---

### Part A: The Most Common Metric Types

Before we look at charts, let's talk about the numbers themselves. Dashboards typically display a few types of metrics.

---

**Counts**
A raw number. How many orders were placed today. How many customers called in. How many units are in inventory. Counts are simple and direct.

*Watch out for:* Context. 500 orders sounds good — but is that up or down from yesterday? From last month?

---

**Percentages and Rates**
These put a count in context. A conversion rate of 3.2% means that out of every 100 website visitors, about 3 made a purchase. A return rate of 8% means 8 out of every 100 products came back.

*Watch out for:* Small sample sizes. A 50% return rate sounds alarming, but if you only sold 2 items, that's just 1 return. Percentages need volume to be meaningful.

---

**Averages**
The total divided by the count. Average order value. Average call handle time. Average rating.

*Watch out for:* Outliers. One extremely large order can pull your average up significantly and make things look better than they are. One very long call can distort handle time. Always ask what's behind the average.

---

**Comparisons (vs. Prior Period)**
Many dashboards show a metric alongside a comparison — "vs. last week" or "vs. same period last year." This is where context lives. A number in isolation is just a number. A number compared to something else is a story.

*Watch out for:* Seasonality. Comparing November sales to October sales may not be fair — November is often higher for retail. Make sure the comparison period makes sense.

---

**Targets and Thresholds**
Some dashboards show a metric in red, yellow, or green based on how it compares to a goal. Red usually means below target. Green means on track or above.

*Watch out for:* Poorly set targets. If the goal was set too low, everything looks green — and that can breed complacency. If targets are unrealistic, everything looks red, which causes unnecessary stress. Know how your targets were set.

---

### Part B: Common Chart Types

---

**Line Chart**
Shows change over time. One dot for each time period, connected by a line.

*Best for:* Trends. Is revenue going up, down, or staying flat? Are support tickets increasing week over week?

*How to read it:* Look at the slope. A line going up means the number is increasing. A line going down means it's decreasing. A flat line means it's staying the same. Look for sharp jumps or drops — those are worth investigating.

---

**Bar Chart**
Compares values across categories. Each bar represents one category.

*Best for:* Comparing things side by side. Sales by product. Revenue by region. Tickets by department.

*How to read it:* Taller bar = higher value. Look for which bars are significantly taller or shorter than the others. The outliers — highest and lowest — are usually where the story is.

---

**Pie Chart**
Shows how a whole is divided into parts. Each slice is a percentage of the total.

*Best for:* Showing proportions. What percentage of our revenue comes from each product line?

*How to read it:* Focus on the largest and smallest slices. Be careful — pie charts with more than five or six slices become very hard to read accurately.

---

**Scorecard or KPI Card**
A single number displayed prominently, often with a comparison to a prior period or a target.

*Best for:* Quick status checks. Total revenue this month. Open tickets right now. Current inventory level.

*How to read it:* Look at the number, then look at the trend indicator (arrow up or down) or the comparison percentage. A KPI card showing "$48,200 ▲ 12% vs. last month" means your metric is $48,200 — up 12% compared to last month.

---

**Table**
Rows and columns of data, like a spreadsheet embedded in the dashboard.

*Best for:* Showing detail. Who are your top 10 customers? Which specific products had returns?

*How to read it:* Look at how the table is sorted. The default sort order usually highlights what the dashboard builder considered most important. Look at the extremes — top row and bottom row.

---

**Heat Map**
A grid where color intensity shows the value. Darker or brighter color usually means higher value.

*Best for:* Spotting patterns across two dimensions at once. What hours of the day + days of the week have the most call volume?

*How to read it:* Look for the darkest clusters. That's where activity is concentrated.

## Filtering and Digging Deeper

**Duration:** 40 minutes
**Format:** Guided instruction + hands-on exercise

---

### Why Filters Matter

A dashboard at its surface level shows you the big picture. Filters let you zoom in. They're the difference between knowing "sales are down 8% this month" and knowing "sales are down 8% this month, but only in the Northeast region, and only for our mid-tier product line."

The second version gives you something to work with.

---

### What Is a Filter?

A filter narrows the data that the dashboard displays. Instead of showing all customers, it shows only customers in a specific city. Instead of showing all time periods, it shows just last quarter. Instead of all product categories, just electronics.

Filters don't delete data. They temporarily limit what's visible so you can focus.

Most dashboards have filters at the top of the page or in a sidebar. Common filter types include:

- **Date range** — Show data for a specific time period
- **Region or location** — Show data for one or more specific areas
- **Product or category** — Narrow down to specific items
- **Customer segment** — Focus on a particular type of customer
- **Employee or team** — Look at one person's or team's performance

---

### Segments vs. Filters

These two terms get used interchangeably, but there's a useful distinction.

A **filter** removes data from the view. If you filter for "Region: West," you only see West data.

A **segment** splits data into groups for comparison. If you segment by region, you might see four bars on a bar chart — one for each region — displayed side by side.

Filtering is for focus. Segmenting is for comparison.

---

### A Practical Filtering Strategy

When something on a dashboard catches your attention — a dip, a spike, an unexpected number — use this three-step approach.

**Step 1: Confirm it's real.**
Apply a date filter to see if the pattern shows up in other time periods too. A single-day spike might be a data glitch. A pattern that repeats is worth taking seriously.

**Step 2: Isolate the variable.**
Filter by one dimension at a time. Start with the most obvious one. If revenue dropped, filter by product first. Then by region. Then by customer type. You're looking for which cut of the data explains the pattern.

**Step 3: Compare the segments.**
Once you've found where the pattern lives, segment that view to compare it against others. If the drop is in one product category, how does that category compare to others over the same period?

## Finding Patterns That Matter

**Duration:** 40 minutes
**Format:** Guided instruction + small group activity

---

### Not Every Blip Is a Signal

One of the most important skills in reading dashboards is knowing the difference between noise and signal. Noise is random variation — the natural ups and downs that happen in any business. Signal is a real pattern that reflects something meaningful.

If your daily sales fluctuate between $8,000 and $12,000 regularly, a day at $9,500 is noise. A three-week slide from $12,000 to $6,000 is a signal.

Acting on noise wastes time and resources. Missing a signal costs you.

---
